## Who we write for

Chief executives, founders, managing directors, vice presidents and directors. They run budgets, teams and P&Ls. They already know what an acquisition, a funding round, a margin and a moat are. They do not need definitions; they need judgment. They read between meetings, on a phone, and stop the moment a paragraph stops paying its way.

They are not impressed by novelty for its own sake. They have sat through many technology hype cycles and many "transformations". They respect writers who show their working, name the trade-off and admit uncertainty.

## Our stance

- **Analytical, not promotional.** We explain what happened and what it means. We do not cheerlead companies, founders or technologies.
- **Sceptical of hype.** A press release is a claim, not a fact. Announced spend is not deployed spend. Targets are not results.
- **Decision-oriented.** Every piece should leave a senior leader with a sharper question, a clearer option or a number worth watching.
- **Second-order.** The first-order story is usually in the headline. Our value is the consequence one or two steps out: who has to respond, what gets cheaper or more expensive, which assumption quietly breaks.
- **Fair to the other side.** State the strongest version of the opposing view before explaining why we lean the other way.

## Rhythm

- Mix short and medium sentences. Average sentence length at or below 22 words; almost nothing above 35.
- One idea per paragraph. Two to four sentences per paragraph.
- Lead each section with its point, then the evidence, then the implication.
- A single short sentence can land a point. Use it sparingly — once or twice per piece.
- Active voice by default. Name the actor: "the board approved", not "it was approved".

## Vocabulary

Prefer the precise operating term: capital allocation, operating leverage, unit economics, payback period, pricing power, switching costs, working capital, gross margin, opex, capex, distribution, regulatory exposure, execution risk, concentration risk.

Use numbers the way an operator does: with the unit, the period and the comparison ("up 12% year on year", "about a quarter of revenue", "the first cut in three years").

Avoid inflated adjectives (massive, huge, incredible, unprecedented) unless the source supports the scale with a number — and then let the number do the work.

## What we never do

- Open with a generic scene-setter about the state of the world, the economy or "the digital age".
- Use AI-filler phrases (see the banned list) or management-consultancy fog.
- Invent quotes, composite characters or hypothetical statistics.
- Present a company's own forward-looking claims as outcomes.
- Close with a bland summary. We close with a decision, a test or a question the reader can act on.
- Use exclamation marks, emoji or rhetorical questions in a row.

## The executive-value test

Every article must clearly answer **at least two** of these questions. If it answers none, it is not ready, however polished it reads.

1. What decision does this change for a senior leader?
2. What would most people get wrong about this?
3. What is the second-order effect?
4. What is the number that actually matters?
5. What would have to be true for the opposite conclusion to hold?
