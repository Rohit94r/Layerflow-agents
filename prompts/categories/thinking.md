## Purpose

A Thinking piece takes a live strategic or leadership question and sharpens it. It does not report news; it uses evidence — research, cases, recent events — to challenge a comfortable assumption and leave the reader with a better way to frame a decision.

- **Route:** `/thinking`
- **Target length:** 900–1,300 words
- **Primary sources:** management research, practitioner essays, earnings-call commentary, surveys, case evidence from news reports.

## Required sections (in this order)

| Key | Default heading | Length | What it must do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `provocation` | The provocation | 120–180 words | State the claim the piece will defend, and why it is uncomfortable or counter-intuitive. Anchor it in one concrete, sourced example or number. |
| `the_tension` | The tension | 170–250 words | Explain the trade-off at the heart of the question: what leaders gain and what they give up. Show why it is genuinely hard, not a straw man. |
| `competing_views` | Two competing views | 200–300 words | Present the two strongest positions fairly, each with its best evidence. Name who tends to hold each view (operators, investors, regulators) where the sources support it. |
| `what_leaders_miss` | What most leaders miss | 170–260 words | The insight: the second-order effect, the hidden cost, the assumption that quietly breaks. This is the reason the piece exists. |
| `practical_implication` | The practical implication | 150–230 words | Turn the insight into something usable: a question to ask in the next leadership meeting, a test to run, a metric to add, a decision rule. |

## Do

- Commit to a position, then steelman the opposite one.
- Use real, sourced examples. Two good examples beat five thin ones.
- Name the trade-off in plain words: "speed for control", "margin for share".
- Make the practical implication specific enough that a reader could act on it this quarter.

## Don't

- Don't write a listicle of tips.
- Don't invent studies, survey figures or anonymous executives ("one CEO told me").
- Don't hedge every sentence; uncertainty belongs in one clear place.
- Don't end with "only time will tell".

## Example opening move (illustrative)

"Most boards treat a cost-cutting programme as a one-off event. The companies that come out of downturns stronger treat it as a capability — and the evidence suggests the difference shows up in margins three years later, not three months."

## Example closing move (illustrative)

"Before the next budget cycle, ask one question of every cost line: if we were starting this business today, would we choose to spend this? The answer will tell you whether you have a cost problem or a strategy problem."
