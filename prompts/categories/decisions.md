## Purpose

A Decisions piece frames a choice that senior leaders are actually facing — build or buy, expand or consolidate, raise or wait — and lays out the options, the criteria that should decide between them, and which option suits which kind of organisation. It does not pretend there is one right answer for everyone.

- **Route:** `/descisions` (the misspelling is the live URL and is kept for SEO compatibility; the display label is "Decisions")
- **Target length:** 900–1,300 words
- **Primary sources:** case evidence from news and filings, company disclosures, research on outcomes of similar decisions, analyst and practitioner commentary.

## Required sections (in this order)

| Key | Default heading | Length | What it must do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `the_question` | The question | 100–160 words | State the decision as a single, specific question, why it is being asked now, and who is asking it. Anchor it in a sourced event or number. |
| `option_a` | Option A | 160–230 words | The first option at its strongest: what it involves, what it costs, what it delivers, and a real example where the sources provide one. Give it a descriptive heading ("Option A: build in-house"). |
| `option_b` | Option B | 160–230 words | The second option, treated with equal fairness and depth. |
| `decision_criteria` | Decision criteria | 150–220 words | Three to five criteria that should decide the choice (time to value, control, cost of reversal, capability, capital). A comparison table across both options is preferred. |
| `risks` | The risks | 120–180 words | The main risk of each option and the early signal that it is materialising. |
| `who_fits` | Which option fits whom | 120–200 words | Match each option to a profile of organisation or situation. End with the condition under which the reader should switch options. |

## Do

- Treat both options fairly. If the evidence clearly favours one, say so — but only after both have been presented at their best.
- Make criteria measurable or at least observable.
- Include the cost of reversing each decision; it is often the deciding factor.
- Use sourced examples of organisations that chose each path.

## Don't

- Don't invent a third "hybrid" option as a cop-out unless the sources discuss it seriously.
- Don't present vendor marketing as evidence.
- Don't use hypothetical numbers.
- Don't conclude with "it depends" without saying on what.

## Example opening move (illustrative)

"Should a mid-sized bank build its own fraud-detection models or buy them? Contoso Bank's decision last month to do the former, after six years of buying, has reopened a question many boards thought was settled."

## Example closing move (illustrative)

"If your fraud losses are below the industry median and your data team is under 20 people, buy. Revisit the decision when either of those numbers changes — not when a vendor contract comes up for renewal."
